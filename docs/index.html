Testing the Efficiency of Football Betting Odds

Bookmakers set odds on every professional football match. Odds can be read as probabilities, so they are a forecast. This project tests that forecast on 16,670 English Premier League and Championship matches from 2000 to 2020. If the market is efficient, outcomes happen as often as the odds say, and no simple rule makes money.

Main resultThe odds are close to calibrated, and a statistical model built from past scores could not improve on them. The market has two consistent weaknesses: long shots are overpriced, and the opening price lags behind news that arrives before kick-off. Neither weakness has faded over 20 years.

Odds Decimal odds of 2.50 return £2.50 for every £1 staked, including the stake. They imply a probability of 1 ÷ 2.50 = 40%. The three probabilities for a match (home win, draw, away win) add up to slightly more than 100%. The excess is the bookmaker's margin, its built-in profit.

The questions

  • Calibration. Do outcomes priced at 30% happen 30% of the time?
  • Bias. Does betting on some kinds of outcome lose less than others?
  • Information. Do prices that move before kick-off move the right way?
  • Forecasting. Can a model built only from past scores beat the market, or add to it?
  • Sharp vs retail. Can the price of a low-margin bookmaker find mistakes at the others?
  • In-play. After half-time, do teams keep scoring at the rate the pre-match odds implied?
  • Change. Has the market become more accurate over 20 years, and is the second division easier to beat?

What I found

FINDING 1

The odds are close to calibrated

Each point groups about 4,000 outcomes with similar prices. Outcomes priced at 64% happened 67% of the time. Outcomes priced at 13% happened 12% of the time. The calibration slope is 1.10 (95% interval 1.06–1.14). A slope above 1 means favourites win slightly more often than priced and long shots slightly less.

FINDING 2

Long shots are overpriced

Betting £1 on every heavy favourite (odds 1–1.5) at the average bookmaker's price lost -2.0%. Betting on every long shot (odds 10+) lost -22.0%. This is the favourite–long-shot bias. It is explained by how bookmakers load their margin: if the margin is assumed to sit mostly on long shots (the “power” method), the calibration slope falls from 1.10 to 1.01.

At the best price across about ten bookmakers, favourites roughly break even. That needs accounts at every bookmaker, and the best price is sometimes a mistake that would be cancelled or limited.

FINDING 3

Margins have fallen by more than half

The average bookmaker's margin fell from 13.0% in 2000 to 5.2% by 2020. Pinnacle, which takes large bets and profits from volume, charges about 2.4%.

FINDING 4

Late price moves point the right way

Pinnacle publishes an opening price and a closing price just before kick-off. Over 4,658 matches (2015 to 2020) the closing odds were the better forecast (log loss 0.9962 against 0.9987). When an outcome's probability rose by at least 3 percentage points before kick-off, a bet at the opening odds returned +11.2% (95% interval +2.5% to +20.7%, 878 bets). When it fell by 3 points, the return was -18.9%.

Nobody knows the move in advance, so this is not a strategy. It shows that new information (team news, injuries, informed money) arrives before kick-off and that the opening price has not absorbed it yet. Several thresholds were tested, so the 3-point result should be read alongside the others: every “shortened” estimate is positive and every “drifted” one is negative.

FINDING 5

A model from past scores cannot beat the market

The model gives each team an attack and a defence rating, fitted to past goals (a Poisson model, as in Dixon & Coles, 1997). It is refitted every week using only earlier matches, and its one setting (how fast old results fade, 730 days) was chosen on seasons before 2008 and never adjusted on the test seasons.

Betting whenever the model rated an outcome at least 5% more likely than the odds did, lost 6.7% per bet at average odds and 3.6% at Pinnacle. At the best price across all bookmakers it returned +0.2%, which is break-even within the uncertainty.

FINDING 6

Pinnacle's price can find generous retail prices, but the evidence is weak

Pinnacle charges a margin of 2.5%; the average bookmaker charges 5.6%. With the margin removed, the two are equally accurate (log loss 1.0028 against 1.0032), and the average adds nothing to Pinnacle.

Retail bookmakers do not all move their prices at once, so sometimes one offers better odds than Pinnacle's fair price. Betting only those prices returned +3.8% per bet (95% interval -0.2% to +7.8%, 4,818 bets over 7,453 matches). When the retail price was at least 2% better, the return was +8.0% (-0.4% to +16.9%).

Every estimate is positive and close to what Pinnacle's price predicted, but every interval touches zero, and most of the profit came in 2012–2014. In practice, retail bookmakers limit customers who do this.

FINDING 7

Margins fell, but the long-shot bias did not

Measured season by season, the calibration slope shows no clear trend: -0.04 per decade (standard error 0.04). Long shots were overpriced in 16 of 20 seasons. Cheaper betting did not make the prices more accurate.

FINDING 8

The Championship is not priced less efficiently

I expected the second division, with less attention and smaller bets, to be easier to beat. It was not. Its calibration slope (1.06, interval 0.99–1.13) is no further from 1 than the Premier League's (1.12, 1.07–1.19), and the model from past scores added nothing in either league.

FINDING 9

In-play: a half-time lead is less safe than a simple model thinks

The data have half-time scores, so I tested a simple way to price a match at half-time: turn the pre-match odds into each team's expected goals, assume the second half is the rest of the same match (56% of goals come after half-time), and add the half-time score.

Teams do not play the second half the same way whatever the score. A team losing by two or more scored +11% more second-half goals than expected; a team winning by two or more scored 7% fewer. The trailing team attacks and the leading team defends.

So the simple model overrates leads. When a narrow pre-match favourite led by one goal at half-time, the model gave it a 76% chance of winning; it won 73% of 3,398 matches. From level at half-time, draws happened 40% of the time against 37% predicted. Adding the score effect, estimated on seasons before 2010, improved the half-time forecasts on the 9,319 later matches (log loss gain 0.0015, 95% interval 0.0006 to 0.0024).

The data have no in-play odds, so this shows what a naive in-play price would get wrong, not whether bookmakers make the same mistake.

What I learned

Score the probabilities

Accuracy (“picked the winner”) hides most of the information. Log loss and the ranked probability score reward a forecast for being confident only when it should be.

Never let the future leak

The model only sees matches before each week, the setting was tuned on separate early seasons, and a test checks this. Without these rules a backtest can look profitable by accident.

Report the uncertainty

Betting returns are noisy. Every number here has a bootstrap interval that resamples whole matches, and several promising-looking strategies turned out to be inside the noise.

How it's built

  • Data: results and odds from up to ten bookmakers per match, from football-data.co.uk.
  • Margin removal: three methods compared (proportional, power, Shin 1993).
  • Model: weekly walk-forward Poisson ratings with time decay, in scikit-learn.
  • Combination: multinomial logistic regression, refitted each season on earlier seasons only.
  • Tests: nine pytest checks, including that the model never sees a match on or after the one it predicts.

Limits

  • English leagues only in the included data. step2_download_more_data.py adds Spain, Germany, Italy and France and the seasons since 2020.
  • The odds are snapshots collected by football-data.co.uk, not every price that was available.
  • This is a study of forecasting, not betting advice. The results show how hard the market is to beat.
