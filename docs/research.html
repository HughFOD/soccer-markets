How Accurate Are Football Betting Odds?

Odds are forecasts: +150 says a result has about a 40% chance. I tested those forecasts on 52,841 matches from the Bundesliga, English Premier League, English Championship, Ligue 1, Serie A and La Liga, every season since 2000.

In shortThe odds are very accurate and a model built from past results couldn't beat them. But they have blind spots: long shots are overpriced, the opening price is slow to react to news, and the simple way to price a game at half-time overrates the team that's ahead.

Bookmakers build more of their cut into long shots, because bettors like them and rarely notice. This favourite–long-shot bias also shows up in horse racing and other betting markets. Taking the best price across about ten bookmakers gets favourites close to breaking even, but that means holding an account with every one of them.

FINDING 5

Prices that move before kick-off move the right way

When a result's chance rose 3+ points before kick-off, a $100 bet at the opening price made +$11. When it fell, the bet lost $15.

Betting early pays off when the price later moves your way

Show the numbers

The data has an opening price and a closing price (just before kick-off) for 31,790 matches from 2012 to 2026, and the closing price was the better forecast. This isn't a strategy, because you can't know the move in advance. It shows that team news and injuries arrive before kick-off and the opening price hasn't caught up yet. Other cut-offs, not just 3 points, point the same way.

FINDING 8

After half-time, the team that's behind scores more than expected

Teams losing by 2+ at half-time scored 15% more in the second half than the odds predicted. Teams winning by 2+ scored 5% fewer.

The simplest way to price a match at half-time is to assume both teams keep scoring at the rate the pre-match odds expected. That's wrong: teams that are behind push forward, and teams that are ahead sit back.

So that simple price overrates the team in front. A slight favourite leading 1–0 at half-time was given a 76% chance of winning and won 72% of the time. Games level at half-time ended in a draw 40% of the time, not the 38% predicted. The data has no live odds, so this shows where a simple live price goes wrong, not whether bookmakers make the same mistake.

Is it really the scoreline? Maybe games with early goals are just more open. To rule that out, I compared the two teams in the same match, which cancels out anything that affects both sides. The pattern holds in all 12 checks: with or without red cards, in both decades, in both English leagues, and after quiet or busy first halves.

What I learned

Judge the chances, not the picks

Counting how often a forecast picks the winner throws away most of the information. An error score rewards a forecast for being confident only when it should be.

Never let the model see the future

The model only uses matches already played, and its setting was chosen on separate early seasons. Without that, a backtest can look profitable by accident.

Always show the uncertainty

Betting results are noisy. Several strategies that looked promising here turned out to be luck once the uncertainty was measured.

How it's built

  • Data: results and odds from up to ten bookmakers per match, from football-data.co.uk.
  • Removing the margin: three methods compared (proportional, power and Shin's 1993 model).
  • Model: team ratings refitted every week on past matches only, in scikit-learn.
  • Uncertainty: 95% ranges from resampling whole matches (the bootstrap).
  • Tests: automated checks, including one that the model never sees a match before predicting it.

Limits

  • The odds are snapshots collected by football-data.co.uk, not every price on offer.
  • The history is European bookmakers; American sites may price differently.
  • This is a study of forecasting, not betting advice. Mostly, it shows how hard the market is to beat.
